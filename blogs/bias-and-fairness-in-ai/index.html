When the Algorithm Is Wrong: Understanding Bias and Fairness in AI

“A system is only as fair as the values baked into it and most of us never get to see those values written down.”

I used to assume that algorithms were neutral. After all, they are just math, right? Numbers do not have opinions. Code does not discriminate. But the more I have studied AI ethics, the more I have come to understand that this assumption is not just wrong it is dangerous.

Bias does not appear in AI systems because someone sat down and decided to be unfair. It seeps in quietly, carried by historical data, encoded in design choices, and amplified by systems that scale to millions of decisions. By the time we notice it, real people have already been harmed.

This post is my attempt to make sense of how bias enters AI systems, why fairness is so difficult to define and achieve, and what we can do — individually and collectively to build something better.

What Is Algorithmic Bias, and Where Does It Come From?

Algorithmic bias refers to systematic and unfair discrimination produced by an AI system. It shows up as outputs that consistently disadvantage certain groups of people by race, gender, age, disability status, or other characteristics in ways that cannot be justified by the task at hand.

But bias does not appear out of nowhere. It has origins, and understanding them is the first step toward addressing it.

Historical Bias in Training Data

Most machine learning models learn from historical data. The problem is that history is not neutral. Historical data reflects the inequalities, prejudices, and structural injustices of the past. When a model is trained on that data, it does not just learn patterns it learns those biases too, and then applies them to future decisions.

A hiring algorithm trained on resumes from the past two decades will observe that most senior roles were held by men. Without careful intervention, it will learn to favour male candidates not because it was programmed to discriminate, but because that is what the data showed.

Measurement Bias

Sometimes the problem is not the data itself, but what we chose to measure. If a model is trained to predict “employee success” but success is measured by promotion rates and promotion rates have historically favoured certain demographic groups then the model is learning a biased proxy, not genuine performance.

Feedback Loops

AI systems deployed in the real world often create feedback loops. A predictive policing algorithm sends more officers to certain neighbourhoods. More policing leads to more arrests in those neighbourhoods. Those arrests feed back into the model as confirmation that those areas are high-crime. The system reinforces its own bias, and the loop becomes self-sustaining.

A Real-World Example: COMPAS and Criminal Justice

Perhaps the most studied and troubling example of algorithmic bias is COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions), a risk assessment tool used in the United States criminal justice system. COMPAS assigns defendants a risk score, a number that is intended to predict the likelihood of reoffending — and judges in some jurisdictions have used this score to inform bail, sentencing, and parole decisions.

In 2016, the investigative journalism outlet ProPublica published an analysis of COMPAS that sent shockwaves through the AI ethics community. Their findings were striking:

  • Black defendants were nearly twice as likely to be falsely flagged as high risk compared to white defendants.
  • White defendants were more likely to be incorrectly labelled as low risk and then went on to reoffend.

The company behind COMPAS argued that the tool was fair because it had equal predictive accuracy across racial groups overall. But ProPublica’s analysis showed that when you broke down the types of errors, the false positive rate (wrongly labelling someone as high risk) was significantly higher for Black defendants. This is a critical distinction: overall accuracy can mask deeply unequal error distributions.

The COMPAS case is not just a technical story. It is a story about what happens when an opaque AI system is used to make life-altering decisions about people without those people having any meaningful ability to understand, challenge, or appeal the result. It touches on algorithmic accountability, transparency, and the limits of technical fixes to deeply human problems.

Why Fairness Is Harder Than It Sounds

Here is the uncomfortable truth that I keep running into in my studies: fairness is not a single, agreed-upon concept. Mathematicians and ethicists have identified multiple definitions of fairness, and they are often mathematically incompatible with each other.

Consider these three definitions:

Fairness Definition What It Means
Demographic parity The model’s positive outcomes are distributed equally across groups
Equal opportunity The model has the same true positive rate across groups
Individual fairness Similar individuals should receive similar predictions

The problem is that you generally cannot satisfy all three at the same time. Optimising for one measure of fairness often comes at the cost of another. This is not a bug that better engineering can fix it is a fundamental tension that requires human judgment about which values matter most in a given context.

This is why AI ethics cannot be reduced to a technical checklist. Deciding what counts as fair is a normative question, a question about values and it belongs in the hands of the communities affected by the system, not just the engineers who built it.

The Role of Ethical AI Frameworks and Governance

So how do we actually move forward? This is where ethical AI frameworks and AI governance become important.

Ethical AI frameworks such as the European Union’s Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, or the OECD Principles on AI provide a set of principles that AI developers and organisations are encouraged to follow. These typically include values like:

  • Fairness and non-discrimination
  • Transparency and explainability
  • Accountability
  • Human oversight
  • Privacy protection

These frameworks are a meaningful step. They signal that the tech industry and governments are taking these issues seriously. But they are largely voluntary, which brings up an important concept from this week’s course material: soft ethics.

Soft Ethics as a Bridge

Soft ethics refers to ethical guidance that operates outside the boundaries of formal law, norms, standards, professional codes of conduct, and voluntary commitments that shape behaviour without legal enforcement. As Floridi (2023) discusses, soft ethics can play a vital role in filling the gaps left by regulation, particularly in fast-moving fields like AI where legislation often struggles to keep pace with technology.

In practice, soft ethics might look like:

  • A company voluntarily publishing its model fairness metrics
  • A data science team adopting an internal ethics review process before deploying a model
  • Professional associations creating codes of conduct that members are expected to follow

Soft ethics is not a replacement for strong regulation but it is better than nothing, and in many cases, it is what we have right now.

What Can Actually Be Done?

Understanding bias is one thing. Addressing it is another. Here are some of the strategies that researchers, practitioners, and policymakers are using to promote fairer AI.

1. Diverse and Representative Data

The most fundamental intervention is to ensure training data is diverse and representative of the population the model will serve. This sounds simple, but it requires deliberate effort and sometimes collecting new data from underrepresented groups.

2. Fairness Audits

Before a model is deployed especially in high-stakes domains like hiring, lending, healthcare, or criminal justice, it should undergo a fairness audit. This means systematically testing for disparate outcomes across demographic groups and documenting the results.

3. Explainability

If a system cannot explain why it made a decision, it is very hard to challenge that decision or identify where bias crept in. Explainability sometimes called interpretability, is increasingly seen as both an ethical requirement and a practical safeguard. Tools like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) are making progress in this area, though there is still a long way to go.

4. Ongoing Monitoring

Bias is not a one-time problem that you fix at deployment. Because of feedback loops and changing data distributions, models can become more biased over time. This means monitoring for fairness needs to be continuous, not a one-off checkbox.

5. Participatory Design

Perhaps the most underused approach is involving affected communities in the design and evaluation of AI systems. People who will be subject to a model’s decisions should have a meaningful voice in determining what fairness looks like for their context. This aligns with what Floridi (2023) describes as human-centric AI, placing human dignity and agency at the centre of how AI is built and governed.

My Honest Reflection

I will be transparent: before this course, I thought about AI bias mostly as a technical problem. Something to fix with better data cleaning or smarter algorithms. What I have come to appreciate is that it is fundamentally a social and ethical problem one that requires social and ethical solutions alongside technical ones.

The COMPAS example sticks with me because it shows what is at stake. When an algorithm makes a mistake in a low-risk app, the consequences are minor. When an algorithm makes a systematic mistake in a courtroom, people lose their freedom. The higher the stakes, the more carefully we need to think about what it means to be fair and the more rigorously we need to hold ourselves and our systems accountable.

Conclusion: Fair Enough Is Not Good Enough

Bias and fairness in AI is not a niche academic topic. It is one of the defining ethical challenges of our time, as AI systems increasingly shape who gets hired, who gets a loan, who gets paroled, and who gets flagged by law enforcement.

The five key concepts I have tried to weave through this post algorithmic bias, algorithmic accountability, soft ethics, explainability, and human-centric AI — are not just buzzwords. They are tools for thinking clearly about what we want from these systems and how to get there.

The good news is that fairness is achievable not perfectly, and not without trade-offs, but meaningfully. It requires diverse teams, honest audits, transparent systems, engaged communities, and a willingness to keep asking hard questions even when the answers are inconvenient.

Because “fair enough” is not good enough when real people’s lives are on the line.

Reference: Floridi, L. (2023). The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence: Principles, Challenges, and Opportunities. Oxford University Press.
